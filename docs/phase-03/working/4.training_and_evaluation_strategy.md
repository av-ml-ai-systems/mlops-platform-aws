# Training and Evaluation Strategy

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document defines the initial training and evaluation strategy for Phase 03 — Training Systems & Model Management of the MLOps Engineering Roadmap.

The objective is to establish a reproducible and technically sound approach for training, evaluating, comparing, and selecting machine learning models for the curated loan-risk dataset.

The strategy will guide subsequent decisions concerning AWS training infrastructure, experiment tracking, model artifact management, validation gates, and model governance.

This document defines the intended methodology, evaluation criteria, provisional thresholds, and unresolved decisions. It does not represent an implemented training pipeline or a production-approved credit decision system.

## 2. Dataset and Business Assumptions

### 2.1 Training Dataset

The initial training dataset produced by Phase 02 is:

`data/processed/loan_risk_dataset/loan_risk_dataset.csv`

The dataset contains 32,581 records and 14 columns. Its target variable is `loan_outcome`, where:

- `0`: Non-default.
- `1`: Default.

The observed target distribution is:

| Target class | Approximate proportion |
|---|---:|
| Non-default (`0`) | 78.18% |
| Default (`1`) | 21.82% |

The dataset is moderately imbalanced. Consequently, accuracy alone is insufficient for evaluating model performance because a model can achieve apparently strong accuracy while failing to identify a substantial proportion of defaults.

The dataset's existing version metadata and lineage information must be retained. Training runs must record the exact dataset version and, where available, its content hash.

### 2.2 Hypothetical Business Objective

For this educational project, the hypothetical objective is to identify loan applicants who may be at risk of default while avoiding an excessive number of false alarms.

This objective is an assumption for developing and demonstrating the ML system. It is not a validated business requirement supplied by a lender.

The model will estimate default risk from the available application data. A prediction must not automatically be interpreted as a final lending decision. In a real lending environment, decisions would require additional business rules, risk policies, regulatory considerations, and appropriate human oversight.

### 2.3 Feature Availability and Data Leakage

Before training, the existing feature-engineering implementation must be assessed for compatibility with the curated dataset.

The feature transformations currently operate on separate domain datasets and use some legacy column names. They must not be assumed to work directly with the curated training schema.

The training design must also establish whether each candidate feature would be available at the time a loan application is evaluated.

In particular, features such as `risk_grade`, `loan_interest_rate`, and `default_history` require investigation to establish their provenance, timing, and potential relationship to the target.

Features that contain information unavailable at prediction time, or that reveal the target directly or indirectly, must not be used without a justified and documented decision.

Data transformations must be fitted exclusively on training data within the applicable training folds. Validation and test data must not influence imputation values, scaling parameters, encoding decisions, feature selection, or other learned preprocessing operations.

## 3. Classification Errors and Business Trade-offs

The model's predictions will be evaluated with `loan_outcome = 1` as the positive class.

| Actual outcome | Model prediction | Classification | Potential consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Default | Non-default | False negative (FN) | A potentially risky applicant is not identified by the model. |
| Default | Default | True positive (TP) | The model correctly identifies an applicant who defaults. |
| Non-default | Default | False positive (FP) | An applicant who would repay is incorrectly flagged as at risk. |
| Non-default | Non-default | True negative (TN) | The model correctly identifies an applicant who does not default. |

False negatives and false positives have different potential consequences.

Reducing false negatives generally improves the model's ability to identify defaults. However, aggressively increasing default detection can also increase false positives, potentially causing more applicants to be flagged incorrectly.

A false positive does not necessarily imply that an applicant will be rejected. Its practical consequence depends on how a lender uses the model's predictions.

Because this project has no validated stakeholder-defined error costs, the relative business cost of false negatives and false positives remains an open question.

## 4. Evaluation Metrics and Provisional Targets

### 4.1 Primary Metric: Recall

Recall measures the proportion of actual defaults correctly identified by the model.

`Recall = TP / (TP + FN)`

Recall is the initial primary metric because the hypothetical objective emphasizes identifying applicants who may default.

A higher recall reduces false negatives, although it may increase false positives.

### 4.2 Secondary Metric: Precision

Precision measures the proportion of applicants predicted to default who actually default.

`Precision = TP / (TP + FP)`

Precision provides context for understanding how many default predictions are correct. It helps evaluate the potential burden of false alarms.

### 4.3 Supporting Metrics

The following metrics will provide additional evidence for model comparison:

- **F1 score:** The harmonic mean of precision and recall. It summarizes their balance mathematically but does not imply that the business costs of false negatives and false positives are equal.
- **PR-AUC:** Summarizes the precision-recall trade-off across classification thresholds. It is particularly useful when the positive class is less frequent than the negative class.
- **ROC-AUC:** Measures the model's ability to distinguish between the two classes across thresholds.
- **Confusion matrix:** Reports true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, making classification errors explicit.
- **Accuracy:** May be reported as a supplementary metric but will not determine model selection on its own.

Metrics must be calculated using consistent definitions and the same evaluation protocol when comparing candidates.

### 4.4 Provisional Performance Thresholds

The following values are initial hypotheses to investigate, not approved business requirements:

| Metric | Provisional target | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Recall | At least 0.80 | Explore whether the model can identify a substantial proportion of defaults. |
| Precision | At least 0.60 | Explore whether default predictions can maintain an acceptable level of correctness. |

These thresholds must be evaluated against validation results. They may be infeasible for some models or may require a trade-off between recall and precision.

They must not be presented as validated lending-industry standards or as evidence that a model is suitable for real credit decisions.

If the thresholds cannot be met simultaneously, the project must document the observed trade-off and revisit the assumptions before selecting a model.

## 5. Training and Evaluation Protocol

### 5.1 Dataset Splitting

The dataset will be divided into training, validation, and test subsets.

A stratified split is the initial approach to consider because the target is binary and the classes are imbalanced. The final split strategy must be confirmed after checking the dataset's characteristics and determining whether other dependencies, such as temporal ordering or related records, require a different approach.

The split proportions and random seed must be recorded in the experiment configuration.

The responsibilities of the subsets are:

- **Training set:** Fit preprocessing operations and model parameters.
- **Validation set:** Compare candidates, tune hyperparameters, and select classification thresholds.
- **Test set:** Perform the final evaluation of the selected model after development decisions have been completed.

The test set must not be repeatedly used to guide model selection or hyperparameter optimization. Its purpose is to provide an independent estimate of the selected model's performance.

Where cross-validation is used, preprocessing must be fitted independently within each training fold.

### 5.2 Baseline Model

The workflow will establish a baseline before relying on more complex models.

A dummy classifier can provide a reference point for understanding the value added by predictive modeling. A simple Logistic Regression model can provide an interpretable, trainable benchmark.

The baseline results will help establish whether more complex candidates produce meaningful improvements under the same evaluation protocol.

### 5.3 Candidate Models

Candidate models will be selected based on the dataset, project objectives, available dependencies, computational constraints, and implementation complexity.

Initial candidates to consider include:

- Logistic Regression.
- Random Forest.
- Gradient-boosted tree models, such as XGBoost or LightGBM, if their dependencies and operational requirements are justified.

The project does not require implementing every candidate. The final candidate set will be defined after confirming preprocessing compatibility and establishing a reliable baseline.

All candidates must use consistent data splits, target definitions, and metric calculations wherever the comparison is intended to be direct.

### 5.4 Hyperparameter Optimization

Hyperparameter optimization will be applied to promising candidates when the expected benefit justifies the additional computational cost.

The optimization process must define and record:

- The candidate model and search space.
- The optimization method.
- The trial budget or stopping conditions.
- The random seed, where applicable.
- The dataset version and split configuration.
- The parameters and results of each trial.
- The software and library versions.

Random search is a possible initial approach because it is straightforward to implement and can explore a defined search space under a controlled budget.

A fixed seed helps make the search reproducible, but it does not guarantee bit-for-bit identical training across different hardware, library versions, or nondeterministic operations.

Hyperparameter optimization and classification-threshold selection must use training and validation data, not the held-out test set.

## 6. Model Selection and Acceptance Criteria

Model selection will follow predefined criteria rather than choosing a model solely because it achieves the highest value for one metric.

The proposed selection process is:

1. Confirm that the dataset and features satisfy the required validation checks.
2. Verify that preprocessing and training complete successfully.
3. Confirm that the resulting model artifact can be serialized, loaded, and used for inference.
4. Compare candidate models using the agreed evaluation protocol.
5. Investigate whether the provisional recall and precision thresholds can be satisfied simultaneously.
6. Examine the confusion matrix, PR-AUC, ROC-AUC, and other supporting evidence.
7. Select a candidate according to the documented criteria and record the rationale.
8. Evaluate the selected candidate on the held-out test set.
9. Record the evaluation results and any remaining limitations.

The initial selection hypothesis is to prioritize recall while requiring an acceptable level of precision. The exact selection rule and acceptable trade-off must be confirmed after observing validation results.

A candidate that fails a hard acceptance requirement must not be selected merely because it performs well on another metric.

If no candidate satisfies the agreed requirements, the outcome must be documented as unresolved rather than forcing a model selection.

Technical acceptance, model approval, and deployment authorization are distinct decisions. Passing evaluation checks does not automatically authorize deployment or production use.

## 7. Reproducibility and Experiment Records

Each training run must produce sufficient information to explain how the model was created and evaluated.

At minimum, experiment records should include:

- Experiment identifier and timestamp.
- Source-code version or commit reference.
- Dataset version and content hash, where available.
- Training, validation, and test split configuration.
- Feature list and preprocessing configuration.
- Model type and hyperparameters.
- Random seeds and optimization settings.
- Python and relevant library versions.
- Training status and execution errors, if any.
- Evaluation metrics and confusion matrix.
- Model artifact location and integrity information.
- Selection outcome and justification.

Model artifacts must be associated with the corresponding experiment records. Where preprocessing is learned from data, the artifacts must preserve the preprocessing required to transform inference inputs consistently with training.

The workflow should support the reconstruction of a training run from its recorded inputs, code, configuration, and software environment to the extent reasonably possible.

Exact numerical reproducibility cannot be guaranteed across all execution environments. The objective is to preserve traceability and make results reproducible as far as the chosen tools and algorithms allow.

## 8. Validation Gates

The training workflow will use explicit validation gates. A gate is a required checkpoint in the workflow; it does not necessarily require a separate Python module or AWS service.

| Gate | Purpose | Expected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Gate 1 — Dataset validation | Confirm that the selected dataset satisfies the training requirements. | Dataset version, schema checks, target checks, and validation results. |
| Gate 2 — Feature validation | Confirm feature compatibility, prediction-time availability, and leakage controls. | Feature definitions, preprocessing configuration, and documented checks. |
| Gate 3 — Training and artifact validation | Confirm that training completes and produces usable artifacts. | Training status, experiment records, and artifact integrity/loadability checks. |
| Gate 4 — Performance validation | Evaluate the model against the agreed metrics and thresholds. | Metrics, confusion matrix, threshold analysis, and evaluation report. |
| Gate 5 — Selection and approval | Record the selected candidate and its justification, separately from deployment authorization. | Comparison results, selection rationale, and approval status where applicable. |
| Gate 6 — Deployment readiness | Assess whether the model can be integrated into an inference service safely and consistently. | Input/output schema, preprocessing compatibility, dependencies, and operational requirements. |

The gates must prevent invalid datasets, incompatible features, failed training runs, unusable artifacts, or models that fail mandatory performance criteria from silently progressing through the workflow.

The implementation may combine related checks where appropriate, provided that their responsibilities and results remain explicit.

## 9. Relationship to Future AWS Deployment and Monitoring

Phase 03 will establish the training, evaluation, experiment-recording, and model-management foundations needed by subsequent phases.

A later phase may integrate the selected model with an AWS inference endpoint, a backend API, and a React frontend. That interface could submit synthetic loan applications and display model predictions.

A short-lived demonstration environment could be used to collect evidence about:

- Prediction endpoint availability and request counts.
- API latency and error rates.
- Input-schema validation and inference failures.
- Resource utilization and relevant operational costs.
- Prediction distributions and feature-distribution changes.

Synthetic requests can test inference behavior and monitoring mechanisms. If synthetic examples have known labels, they can also test metric-calculation and evaluation workflows. However, synthetic data alone does not establish real-world predictive performance.

Feature drift refers to changes in the distribution of model inputs. Concept drift concerns changes in the relationship between inputs and the target. Feature-distribution monitoring alone cannot establish concept drift; reliable outcome labels and appropriate analysis are generally needed.

A future retraining workflow may be demonstrated, but retraining must not automatically promote a new model. A newly trained candidate must pass the applicable validation, evaluation, and approval requirements.

These deployment, monitoring, and retraining activities are future roadmap objectives, not implementation requirements for the current strategy document.

## 10. Decisions, Assumptions, and Open Questions

### 10.1 Initial Decisions

- The curated Phase 02 loan-risk dataset is the starting point for training.
- `loan_outcome = 1` is the positive class.
- Recall is the initial primary evaluation metric.
- Precision is the initial secondary metric.
- PR-AUC, F1 score, ROC-AUC, and the confusion matrix provide supporting evidence.
- A stratified split is the initial approach to evaluate, subject to dataset-dependency checks.
- Hyperparameter optimization will use a controlled search space and budget.
- Training runs must retain dataset lineage, configuration, software information, metrics, and model artifacts.
- Model evaluation, approval, and deployment are separate stages.

### 10.2 Provisional Assumptions

- Recall of at least 0.80 and precision of at least 0.60 are hypotheses to assess, not approved requirements.
- The educational scenario prioritizes identifying potential defaults while limiting false alarms.
- Candidate models will be selected based on empirical results and implementation constraints.
- Reproducibility means traceability and consistent results where feasible, not a guarantee of bit-for-bit identity across all environments.

### 10.3 Open Questions

The following decisions remain open and must be addressed before or during implementation:

1. Are the provisional recall and precision thresholds feasible and appropriate after validation?
2. What final dataset split strategy and proportions should be used?
3. Which existing feature transformations are compatible with the curated training schema?
4. Which features are available at prediction time, and which require exclusion or further investigation?
5. Which candidate models and dependencies should be included in the initial experiment?
6. What hyperparameter optimization method and computational budget are appropriate?
7. How should the final classification threshold be selected and documented?
8. What model artifact format and experiment-record structure should be used?
9. What approval criteria will be required before deployment?
10. Which AWS services and resource lifecycle controls will support training and model management?

These questions will inform the subsequent AWS architecture, reproducibility and infrastructure-lifecycle design, architectural decision records, and final implementation sequence.

## 11. Completion Criteria

This strategy is ready to support the next design tasks when:

- The training and evaluation objectives are documented.
- Classification errors and their potential consequences are understood.
- Primary, secondary, and supporting metrics are defined.
- Provisional thresholds are explicitly distinguished from validated requirements.
- The intended dataset-splitting and leakage-control approach is documented.
- Candidate-model evaluation and selection principles are established.
- Reproducibility and experiment-record requirements are defined.
- Validation gates and their responsibilities are documented.
- Remaining assumptions and architectural questions are recorded for resolution in later tasks.

Completing this document establishes the strategy for Phase 03. It does not claim that training, evaluation, model registration, AWS infrastructure, or deployment has already been implemented or validated.
